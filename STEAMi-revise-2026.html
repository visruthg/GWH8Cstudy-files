1. STEAM

the problem-solving framework

STEAM stands for Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts and Mathematics. In aerospace, these five areas operate together rather than as isolated school subjects.

  • Science provides the principles of forces, motion, airflow, energy and materials.
  • Technology provides tools for modelling, sensing, measurement and communication.
  • Engineering converts requirements into designs and tests them.
  • Arts supports visual communication and design thinking.
  • Mathematics allows engineers to quantify dimensions, ratios, speed, angles, area and experimental results.
Worked exampleIf a team is asked to improve a paper aircraft: science helps explain airflow, mathematics helps compare measurements, engineering guides the redesign, technology can support data collection, and visual communication (arts) allows the team to explain the design.
Key idea — often examinedA strong engineering solution must satisfy a purpose and work within constraints. A design that looks attractive but performs poorly is not automatically a successful engineering design.

Evolution of flight technology

Progress in aerospace depends on aerodynamics, propulsion, materials, control, navigation and engineering, all improving together.

Go deeper — why "attractive but poor performance" isn't good engineering

An IGCSE-style examiner may ask you to justify a design choice. A design that "looks like a jet fighter" but cannot fly straight has failed its purpose. Always tie your justification back to the stated performance target (distance, stability, flight time, etc.), not to appearance.

2. Aviation & Aircraft Basics

history & types of aircraft

Aviation is the field concerned with the design, development, operation and use of aircraft. It includes passenger aircraft, helicopters, military aircraft, unmanned aircraft and lighter-than-air vehicles. Aeronautics focuses particularly on flight within Earth's atmosphere.

A brief history of flight

Human flight developed through a sequence of ideas, not a single invention. Early attempts were influenced by observations of birds and the behaviour of air. Practical flight required understanding buoyancy, aerodynamics, propulsion, materials and control.

  • 1783 — The Montgolfier brothers demonstrated a hot-air balloon capable of carrying people. This showed that buoyancy alone could support human flight.
  • 1903 — Orville and Wilbur Wright achieved controlled, powered airplane flight at Kitty Hawk. This proved that successful powered flight requires more than an engine: the aircraft must also be aerodynamically designed and controllable.
  • 20th century — Improvements in structures, engines, materials, aerodynamics, navigation and control let aircraft fly farther, faster and higher. Jet propulsion opened the way to much higher speeds.
  • Today — Modern aircraft combine aerodynamics with digital computers, sensors, communication, navigation and advanced materials.
Exam tipIf asked "what did the Wright Flyer prove?", don't just say "it could fly" — the marking point is usually that it showed flight needs aerodynamic design + control, not just an engine.

Types of aircraft

TypeHow it stays upKey feature
Fixed-wingForward motion creates airflow over wings that remain fixed relative to the aircraft, allowing aerodynamic forces to develop.Generally requires forward motion for sustained flight.
Rotary-wingRotating blades (a rotor) generate aerodynamic forces.Can hover and take off/land vertically; the rotor must both generate and control the aerodynamic forces.
Lighter-than-airRelies mainly on buoyancy.A hot-air balloon rises when the heated air inside becomes less dense than the surrounding atmosphere — it depends on the relationship between the vehicle's overall density and the density of the surrounding air.
Go deeper — why fixed-wing aircraft need forward motion

A fixed wing only generates useful lift when air flows over its aerofoil shape fast enough. Without forward motion (or a powerful enough airflow from elsewhere, as in a rotor), there isn't enough relative airflow to generate lift — this is why fixed-wing aircraft need a runway, and rotary-wing aircraft don't.

3. Parts of an Aircraft

systems thinking

An aircraft is an integrated system. Each major component performs a function, but changing one component can affect the performance of others. This systems view is an important step beyond simply memorising aircraft parts.

Wings

The wings provide most of the lift in a conventional fixed-wing aircraft. Their area, profile, structure and interaction with airflow influence aerodynamic performance. Wing design must balance lift capability, drag, strength, mass and the aircraft's intended mission.

Fuselage

The fuselage forms the main body of the aircraft and may contain passengers, cargo, equipment, fuel systems and other components. Its shape affects internal capacity, structural requirements and aerodynamic resistance.

Cockpit

The cockpit is the control and monitoring area. Instruments and displays provide information about aircraft condition, navigation and flight. The design must allow the operator to understand information and control the aircraft effectively.

Tail / empennage

The empennage contributes to stability and control. It includes the horizontal and vertical tail surfaces and supports control surfaces such as the elevator and rudder. Tail geometry affects how the aircraft responds to disturbances and control inputs.

Landing gear

Landing gear supports the aircraft on the ground and absorbs loads during ground operations, take-off and landing. Its structure must be strong enough for expected loads, but the additional mass and aerodynamic effects must also be considered.

Engine and propulsion system

The propulsion system produces thrust and supplies the energy needed for sustained forward motion. Its mass, thrust capability, efficiency and integration with the aircraft all influence performance.

Systems thinking — a favourite exam angleSuppose an engineer increases wing size. The larger wing may provide more lifting surface, but it may also change drag, structural requirements and mass. Therefore, an engineering change should be judged by its overall effect, not by one advantage alone.

4. The Four Forces of Flight

lift · weight · thrust · drag

Flight results from the interaction between an aircraft, the surrounding air, gravity and its propulsion system. Saying "the engine makes the aircraft fly" is incomplete: the propulsion system primarily provides thrust, while aerodynamic surfaces interact with the airflow to produce forces.

Lift — an aerodynamic force generated by the interaction between an aircraft and the surrounding airflow. In straight flight it acts generally upward and helps oppose weight.
Weight — the gravitational force acting on the aircraft, toward the centre of the Earth. Determined by aircraft mass and the gravitational field.
Thrust — the force produced by the propulsion system, generally in the direction of motion, used to overcome drag and change speed.
Drag — the aerodynamic force that opposes motion through the air. Aircraft designers manage drag because excessive drag increases the thrust required for a desired speed.

Force balance and net force

Net force = 0  ⇒  constant velocity (Newton's First Law)
  • If lift = weight and thrust = drag, the four forces are balanced and the aircraft flies at a constant velocity.
  • If thrust > drag, there is a forward net force and the aircraft accelerates forward.
  • If lift > weight, there is an upward net force and the aircraft accelerates upward.
Critical idea — commonly testedZero net force does not mean zero velocity. An aircraft can travel at a constant velocity while the opposing forces balance. A change in velocity requires acceleration, and acceleration requires a non-zero net force.

The four-force model is a simplification. In real manoeuvres (climbing, descending, turning) forces act in different directions, so engineers use vector reasoning and more detailed models.

Go deeper — the chain of cause and effect

Useful mental model: propulsion changes motion → motion produces relative airflow → airflow interacts with aircraft surfaces → aerodynamic forces develop → the balance of forces determines changes in motion. Gravity produces weight; control surfaces modify aerodynamic forces so that attitude and direction can be changed.

5. Aerodynamics & Aerofoils

how a wing generates lift

Aerodynamics is the study of how air moves around objects and how that interaction produces forces and affects motion. It is central to aircraft design because aircraft must generate useful aerodynamic forces while limiting unwanted resistance and maintaining controllability.

What is an aerofoil?

An aerofoil (airfoil) is a specially shaped surface designed to interact with moving air and generate an aerodynamic force. A wing cross-section is an example of an aerofoil.

Leading edge — the front portion that first encounters the airflow.
Trailing edge — the rear portion where the upper and lower surfaces meet.
Chord line — a reference line connecting the leading and trailing edges.
Camber — the curvature of the aerofoil profile relative to its chord line.
Angle of attack — the angle between the chord line and the relative wind (oncoming airflow).
Centre of pressure — the point where the total aerodynamic force can be considered to act.

A simple explanation of lift

Air flowing over the curved upper surface of an aerofoil travels differently to air flowing beneath it, changing the surrounding pressure field. The overall pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces, combined with the airflow's downward deflection, produces the upward aerodynamic force we call lift.

Factors that influence flight

  • Shape or profile affects the airflow around the aircraft.
  • Wing area changes the amount of surface interacting with the air.
  • Aircraft speed changes the aerodynamic conditions.
  • Air conditions such as temperature and density affect how the airflow behaves.
  • Angle and direction of airflow relative to the aircraft surface also matters.
Engineering trade-off — likely "evaluate" questionAircraft designers rarely optimise one quantity without affecting others. A wing that performs well for one mission may not be ideal for another. Engineers therefore define a performance goal, identify constraints, test alternatives, and choose a design based on evidence.
Go deeper — don't treat these factors as isolated rules

A design change can affect several outcomes at once. For example, changing a wing may influence lift, drag, stability and structural requirements at the same time — this is exactly the kind of "systems thinking" IGCSE examiners like to test with an "evaluate" or "discuss" question.

6. Newton's Laws of Motion

the physics behind flight

Newton's laws provide a physics framework for understanding how forces influence aircraft motion.

First Law — Inertia

An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity unless an unbalanced external force acts on it. For an aircraft, balanced forces can allow constant velocity — a force is not required simply to maintain motion when resistance and driving forces are balanced.

Second Law — F = ma

F = m × a

Newton's Second Law connects net force, mass and acceleration. For a fixed mass, increasing net force increases acceleration. For a fixed net force, increasing mass decreases acceleration.

Worked exampleA model aircraft of mass 2 kg experiences a net force of 8 N.
a = F/m = 8/2 = 4 m/s².
If the same force acts on a 4 kg model: a = 8/4 = 2 m/s² — the heavier aircraft accelerates less under the same net force.

Third Law — Action and reaction

Interacting bodies exert forces on each other. In propulsion, the engine or propeller system accelerates air or exhaust in one direction, and an associated reaction force contributes to thrust on the aircraft.

How the three laws connectThe First Law describes the condition of constant velocity when net force is zero; the Second Law quantifies how net force changes motion; the Third Law helps explain the force interactions involved in propulsion.

7. Aircraft Stability & the Three Axes

roll · pitch · yaw

Aircraft move in three dimensions. To describe their rotational motion clearly, engineers use three imaginary axes passing through the aircraft. Each rotational movement occurs about one of these axes.

AxisRotation nameRuns throughEffect
Longitudinal axisRollNose to tailWings change their relative orientation.
Lateral / transverse axisPitchWingtip to wingtipChanges the aircraft's nose-up or nose-down attitude.
Vertical axisYawTop to bottom, through the centre of gravityChanges the direction in which the aircraft's nose points.

Stability vs. control

Stability — the tendency of an aircraft to maintain or return toward a desired flight condition after a disturbance.
Control — the ability to intentionally change the aircraft's attitude or direction.

Stability and control are related but different. Changes in airflow or other external effects can disturb an aircraft. Excessive resistance to change can make an aircraft difficult to manoeuvre, while insufficient stability can make the aircraft difficult to manage.

Exam tip — a very common 3-mark questionGive a full definition of both stability and control, and name all three axes with their correct rotation names — that's usually worth one mark each.
Go deeper — balancing stability and control

Good aircraft design requires control authority sufficient to perform intended manoeuvres while retaining suitable stability. This is a design balance rather than a choice between one or the other — too much stability makes an aircraft sluggish to manoeuvre; too little makes it hard to fly predictably.

8. Primary Control Surfaces

ailerons · elevator · rudder

An important engineering distinction: a control surface does not directly "become" the aircraft's motion. Moving a control surface changes airflow and aerodynamic forces; those forces create moments that rotate the aircraft.

Control surfaceRotation producedLocationMechanism
AileronsRollOuter rear sections of the wingsOperate differentially so the aerodynamic effect on one wing differs from the other, producing a rolling moment about the longitudinal axis.
ElevatorPitchHorizontal tailChanging its position alters the aerodynamic force and pitching moment produced by the tail, influencing nose-up or nose-down attitude.
RudderYawVertical tailIts movement changes the aerodynamic force acting on the vertical tail, producing a yawing moment about the vertical axis.
Exam tip — easy marks lost hereAlways say ailerons → roll, elevator → pitch, rudder → yaw — never mix these up. A common mistake is swapping elevator and rudder.

Control and stability together

Good aircraft design requires control authority sufficient to perform intended manoeuvres while retaining suitable stability. This is a design balance rather than a choice between one or the other.

Go deeper — why control surfaces work "indirectly"

Examiners like to test the idea that a control surface does not directly rotate the aircraft. The chain is: control surface deflects → local airflow and pressure distribution changes → aerodynamic force on that surface changes → this creates a moment (turning effect) about the relevant axis → the aircraft rotates. If asked to "explain" how an aileron causes roll, include this chain rather than just "it moves the wing".

9. Mach Number

speed compared with sound

Mach number is a dimensionless ratio comparing an object's speed with the local speed of sound.

M = V ÷ a

where V is the object's speed and a is the local speed of sound.

RegimeConditionDescription
SubsonicM < 1Below the local speed of sound.
Transonicaround M = 1Airflow can include regions approaching sonic conditions.
SupersonicM > 1Above the local speed of sound.
HypersonicM > 5A very high-speed regime with strong compressibility effects.
Worked exampleAn aircraft travels at 680 m/s while the local speed of sound is 340 m/s.
M = V ÷ a = 680 ÷ 340 = 2.
The aircraft is therefore at Mach 2, in the supersonic regime.
Worked example — ratio, not fixed speedIf V = 510 m/s and a = 340 m/s, M = 1.5. If the aircraft's speed stays the same but the local speed of sound changes (e.g. with altitude or temperature), its Mach number also changes, because Mach number is a ratio, not a fixed speed.
Why Mach number matters — the "explain" part of the questionMach number is more informative than raw speed alone in high-speed flight because the speed of sound changes with atmospheric conditions. As aircraft speed approaches and exceeds the speed of sound, air compressibility and shock waves can significantly affect aerodynamic behaviour, including lift and drag.
Go deeper — why low-speed air can be treated as incompressible

At lower speeds, air can often be treated as approximately incompressible for introductory analysis. At high Mach numbers, density changes associated with pressure disturbances become increasingly important. Shock waves can form and can cause rapid changes in pressure, temperature and flow direction.

10. Wind Tunnel & Fair Testing

investigating aerodynamics

A wind tunnel is a facility that produces controlled airflow around a model so engineers can investigate aerodynamic behaviour. It allows designers to study how changes in shape or flow conditions influence performance before relying entirely on full-scale flight tests.

What can be investigated?

  • Flow patterns around a model.
  • Effects of wing or body shape.
  • Forces associated with airflow speed.
  • Stability-related behaviour.
  • Differences between alternative designs.

Designing a fair investigation

A good experiment changes one planned independent variable while keeping important controlled variables similar. The dependent variable is the quantity measured or observed. Repeated trials increase confidence in the pattern, while evidence provides the basis for a conclusion.

Worked exampleTo compare two wing shapes: use models with comparable dimensions, keep the airflow condition and test setup consistent, measure an agreed performance variable, repeat the tests and compare the results. If the evidence is inconsistent, the correct response may be to investigate the cause rather than simply choose a winner.

Limitations of experiments

No experiment is perfect. Measurement uncertainty, model scale, airflow disturbance, construction differences and limited trials can affect results. Recognising these limitations is part of scientific evaluation.

Exam tipIf asked to "state a limitation" of an experiment, name a specific source of error (e.g. "limited number of trials reduces confidence in the pattern") rather than a vague "human error".

11. Paper Plane Engineering & Think & Apply Project

turning folding into engineering

Paper-plane engineering turns classroom aerodynamics into a structured investigation. The goal is not simply to fold a plane but to define a performance target, build a controlled prototype, collect evidence and improve the design.

  1. Define the target — decide what "better" means before testing. A target might be greater distance, longer flight time, improved stability, straighter flight, or a combination of goals.
  2. Design and build — choose a design and build it carefully. Symmetry matters because unequal folds can introduce unwanted turning or rolling behaviour.
  3. Test under repeatable conditions — use a similar launch position, launch technique and environment. The more consistent the test conditions, the more confidently results can be compared.
  4. Observe and measure — record useful evidence such as distance, flight time, direction and stability. Decide in advance how the measurement will be made.
  5. Change one important feature — change one major feature at a time, such as wing shape, wing size, centre of mass, or another defined variable. If several features are changed simultaneously, identifying the cause of an improvement becomes difficult.
  6. Improve, retest and justify — use evidence to select the next design change. Test the modified design again. Finally, justify the design decision by explaining the performance target, evidence, trade-off and limitations.
Engineering trade-offImagine Design A travels farther but Design B remains more stable. Neither is automatically "the best" — if maximum distance is the target, A may be preferable; if predictable straight flight is the target, B may be preferable. Engineering decisions depend on requirements.

Variables to investigate

  • Wing shape and profile
  • Wing size or area
  • Balance and centre of mass
  • Fold accuracy and symmetry
  • Launch angle
  • Distance travelled
  • Flight time
  • Stability and direction

Think & Apply Project — task checklist

What the final write-up needs
  1. Choose one paper-plane design and define one measurable performance target.
  2. Draw and label the aircraft; identify the major design feature you believe controls its performance; write a prediction about what will happen if that feature is changed.
  3. Plan a fair test: state the variable you will change, the measurement you will record, the conditions you will keep constant, and the number of trials you will perform.
  4. Conduct the test, examine the evidence, and identify at least one limitation.
  5. Redesign the aircraft based on the evidence. Explain whether the redesign improved the chosen performance target and identify any trade-off introduced by the change.

Final conclusion should answer four questions: What did you change? What happened? What evidence supports your conclusion? What would you test next?

13. Revision Questions

try first, then reveal the answer

1. An aircraft is flying at a constant velocity. Explain how the four forces acting on the aircraft are balanced. What would happen if thrust becomes greater than drag and lift becomes greater than weight? 5 marks

Show answer
  • When an aircraft is flying at a constant velocity, the four forces are balanced.
  • Lift acts upward and weight acts downward. When lift equals weight, there is no vertical acceleration.
  • Thrust acts forward and drag acts backward. When thrust equals drag, there is no forward acceleration.
  • Therefore, the net force is approximately zero, so the aircraft continues moving at a constant velocity.
  • If thrust becomes greater than drag, there is a forward net force and the aircraft accelerates forward. If lift becomes greater than weight, there is an upward net force and the aircraft accelerates upward.

2. Explain the difference between stability and control in an aircraft. Also state the three axes of aircraft motion and the movement associated with each axis. 3 marks

Show answer

Stability is the ability of an aircraft to maintain or return to its desired flight condition after a disturbance. Control is the ability of the pilot or control system to intentionally change the aircraft's motion or attitude.

  • Longitudinal axis → Roll
  • Lateral/Transverse axis → Pitch
  • Vertical axis → Yaw

3. An aircraft is travelling at 680 m/s while the local speed of sound is 340 m/s. Calculate its Mach number and identify the flight regime. Explain why Mach number is important in high-speed flight. 3 marks

Show answer

M = Aircraft speed ÷ Speed of sound = 680 ÷ 340 = 2. The aircraft is travelling at Mach 2, in the supersonic flight regime.

Mach number is important because it compares an aircraft's speed with the local speed of sound. At high speeds, effects such as compressibility and shock waves can significantly affect aerodynamic performance.

4. State Newton's Second Law of Motion and write its mathematical equation. 2 marks

Show answer

Newton's Second Law states that the acceleration of an object depends on the net force acting on it and its mass.

F = ma

Where F = net force, m = mass, a = acceleration.

5. What is a wind tunnel? State one reason why aerospace engineers use wind tunnels. 2 marks

Show answer

A wind tunnel is a controlled facility in which air is passed over a model or component to study its aerodynamic behaviour.

Aerospace engineers use wind tunnels to study airflow and aerodynamic forces such as lift and drag, and to evaluate aircraft or wing designs before testing them on a full-scale aircraft.
