9.1 What do we know about plants?

Starting point · thinking like a scientist

Plants are producers — organisms that make their own food rather than eating other organisms. They do this through photosynthesis, using light energy to build glucose from carbon dioxide and water. Almost every food chain on Earth begins with a plant (or another producer) trapping light energy.

Two features come up again and again in this chapter, so it is worth fixing them early:

  • Cell walls — made of cellulose, they give plant cells support and protection, and make plant tissue tougher for animals to digest (which is why herbivores need specialised guts and teeth).
  • Stomata — tiny pores, mostly on the lower surface of a leaf, that open and close to let gases move in and out for photosynthesis and respiration.
Reflective routine used in your notes What do I think I know? → What puzzles me? → How can I explore it? This "K‑W‑L" style routine is a Thinking & Working Scientifically habit: it is worth using at the start of every new topic, not just this one.
Go deeper — autotrophs vs heterotrophs

Autotrophic nutrition (plants, algae, some bacteria) means making complex organic molecules from simple inorganic ones (CO₂ + H₂O) using an energy source (light). Heterotrophic nutrition (animals, fungi) means taking in ready-made organic molecules by feeding on other organisms. Fungi are heterotrophs too, but they digest food externally and absorb the products — a detail that often appears in "classify this organism" exam questions.

Another reflective routine — compass points Need to know / Excited about / Worried about / Suggestion for how to explore it. Another way to open a new topic: name what you still need to find out, what interests you about it, any concerns (e.g. "is it safe?"), and one concrete way to investigate — such as a lab experiment.

Worked example — reading a real crop-yield graph

Your coursebook (Fig 9.1 B) plots global crop yields from 1961 to 2018 in tonnes per hectare. Potatoes and bananas are the highest-yielding crops shown (around 20 t/ha by 2018); cocoa beans, beans and peas sit lowest (under 5 t/ha throughout). This is the kind of graph IGCSE-style questions love to combine with a percentage-change calculation:

Percentage change — the formula to memorise percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100

Example: potato yield rises to about 20 t/ha by 2018. If an earlier reading was roughly 18.1 t/ha, the percentage increase is (20 − 18.1) ÷ 18.1 × 100 ≈ 10.4%. Always show the substitution step, not just the final number — that is usually where a mark is awarded even if your final answer is slightly off due to graph-reading.

9.2 Photosynthesis

The core equation of this whole chapter

Definition Photosynthesis is the process by which plants make carbohydrates (glucose) from raw materials, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.
carbon dioxide  +  water  ⟶  glucose  +  oxygen light energy, trapped by chlorophyll
6CO2 + 6H2O  ⟶  C6H12O6 + 6O2 light / chlorophyll shown above the arrow

The reaction happens inside chloroplasts. Their internal structure is built for exactly this job:

  • Grana — stacks of thylakoid membranes containing chlorophyll, which traps light energy.
  • Lamellae — membranes that connect one granum to another.
  • Stroma — the fluid surrounding the grana, containing the enzymes that build glucose.
  • Starch granules — store surplus glucose (converted to starch) for later use.
  • Ribosomes — carry out protein synthesis inside the chloroplast.
Go deeper — light-dependent vs light-independent reactions

Photosynthesis actually happens in two linked stages, both still part of photosynthesis (a common mix-up is to call the second stage "respiration" — it isn't):

  • Light-dependent reactions — happen in the thylakoid membranes (grana). Light is absorbed by chlorophyll and used to split water and generate energy (ATP).
  • Light-independent reactions — happen in the stroma. The energy from the first stage is used to combine carbon dioxide with a starting molecule to build glucose. They don't need light directly, but they depend on products from the light-dependent stage.

At Core level you mainly need the word/symbol equation and the role of chlorophyll and light; the two-stage detail is useful background for Extended-level "explain why" questions.

Photosynthesis matters beyond the individual plant: it is the ultimate source of food and oxygen for almost all life, which is why producers sit at the base of every food chain, and why nearly all biomass on Earth — wood, cereal grains, flowers, and everything that eats them — traces back to it.

What happens to the glucose that is made?

A plant does not just make glucose and stop — it puts it to three main uses:

  • Aerobic respiration — roughly half of the glucose made is respired straight away to release energy for the cell's other reactions.
  • Storage as starch — glucose is soluble and cannot be stored efficiently, so it is joined into insoluble starch and stored in leaves (enough for several days without light) or in organs such as potato tubers.
  • Building other molecules — glucose is the starting material for making other carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and chlorophyll itself.

The number of chloroplasts in a cell is a rough guide to how much photosynthesis that cell can carry out — a cell with no chloroplasts (like a root hair cell) cannot photosynthesise at all.

9.3 Evidence for photosynthesis: testing for starch

The iodine test — learn this as an exact sequence

Glucose made by photosynthesis is quickly converted to starch for storage. Since starch is easy to test for, its presence in a leaf is used as indirect evidence that photosynthesis has occurred there.

  1. De-starch the plant beforehand by leaving it in the dark for 24–48 hours, so any existing starch is used up.
  2. Kill the leaf by dipping it in boiling water for about 30 seconds — this stops enzyme activity and softens the leaf.
  3. Decolourise by boiling the leaf in ethanol (in a test tube standing in a water bath, never over a naked flame — ethanol is flammable) until the chlorophyll dissolves out and the leaf turns white.
  4. Soften the brittle leaf by dipping it briefly back into hot water.
  5. Test by spreading the leaf flat and covering it with iodine solution.
ResultMeaning
Blue-blackStarch is present — photosynthesis has taken place there
Stays orange-brownNo starch — that area has not photosynthesised (e.g. it was shaded, or is a variegated white patch with no chlorophyll)
Go deeper — classic variations on this experiment

Variegated leaf (green and white patches): only the green (chlorophyll-containing) areas turn blue-black — shows chlorophyll is needed.

Partly covered leaf (black paper over part of it, left in light): only the uncovered area turns blue-black — shows light is needed.

Leaf with no CO₂ (enclosed with soda lime, which absorbs CO₂): stays orange-brown — shows carbon dioxide is needed.

Three real investigation plans (Salma, Dina and Abdul)

Your coursebook sets exactly this test up as three separate investigations, each starting with a plant kept in the dark for at least 3 days first (so you know it has no leftover starch), then testing a leaf with iodine after 24 hours in the new condition. Comparing the three side by side is a good way to see how the "kill → decolourise → soften → test" method stays fixed while only the set-up changes:

InvestigatorQuestionWhat they changeResult if their factor is needed
SalmaDo plants need chlorophyll?Uses a plant with two-coloured (variegated) leavesOnly the green parts of the leaf turn blue-black
DinaDo plants need light?Covers one leaf with light-blocking foilOnly the uncovered part turns blue-black
AbdulDo plants need carbon dioxide?Encloses one leaf in a bag with a CO₂-absorbing chemicalThat leaf stays orange-brown; a leaf left in normal air (the control) turns blue-black

Notice each plan has a control — a leaf or plant not missing the tested factor — so the result can be attributed to the one thing that was changed. That is exactly what an exam means by "a fair test".

9.4 Evidence of photosynthesis: oxygen bubbles

Pondweed experiments and limiting factors

Aquatic plants such as Elodea (pondweed) release the oxygen they produce as visible gas bubbles, so counting bubbles — or collecting the gas — gives a direct, real-time measure of the rate of photosynthesis.

Two common methods:

  • Count the number of bubbles released per minute from the cut end of the stem.
  • Collect the gas in an inverted funnel over the pondweed, feeding into an upturned, water-filled test tube — measure the volume of gas collected in a set time.
Limiting factors Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature can each hold back the rate of photosynthesis. Increasing one factor raises the rate — until a different factor becomes the one holding it back, at which point the graph plateaus.
Go deeper — the inverse square law with light intensity

Moving the lamp is a standard way to change light intensity in this experiment. Light intensity is proportional to 1/distance². This means halving the distance to the lamp doesn't double the light intensity — it quadruples it. Exam questions sometimes ask you to use this relationship to calculate a new light intensity after the distance is changed.

  • Test 2 is often a little lower than Test 1 at the same distance — possibly the plant needs longer to adjust to a new light intensity, or the cut stem is slowly healing over and releasing fewer bubbles over time.
  • A thermometer in the beaker matters because the lamp itself warms the water — without checking, a change in bubble rate could be due to temperature rising rather than light intensity changing, which would ruin the fair test.

A second way to test for CO₂: hydrogencarbonate indicator

Hydrogencarbonate indicator solution is orange in normal air. It changes colour depending on the amount of dissolved carbon dioxide:

If pondweed is left in a sealed tube of the indicator in good light, it photosynthesises faster than it respires, removes CO₂ overall, and the indicator turns purple. Left in the dark, the plant only respires (adding CO₂) and the indicator turns yellow.

9.5 The need for minerals

Mineral ions, transport mechanisms, and root hair cells

Photosynthesis makes glucose, but plants need extra mineral ions — water-soluble substances absorbed from the soil — to build other essential molecules that carbohydrates alone cannot provide.

Mineral ionNeeded to makeDeficiency symptom
Nitrate (NO₃⁻)Amino acids / proteins, which control reactions (including photosynthesis) and build cell structuresPoor/stunted growth; older leaves turn yellow
Magnesium (Mg²⁺)Chlorophyll — needed in every new chloroplast, and to replace worn-out chlorophyllLeaves turn pale or yellow
Where do the minerals come from? Minerals are dissolved in soil water and absorbed through the root hair cells, then carried around the plant in the transport tissues. Most nitrates come from decomposers breaking down dead organisms and waste — but legumes (peas, beans, clover) are the exception: they host nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules, so they don't rely on soil nitrate. This is why legumes grow well in nitrate-poor soil, and why farmers plant them to help fertilise a field naturally for the next crop.

Transport: passive vs active

Passive transport needs no energy — particles move down a concentration gradient (high → low):

  • Diffusion — net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.
  • Osmosis — diffusion of water molecules only, from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, across a partially permeable membrane.

Active transport moves substances from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration — against the gradient — which needs energy released by respiration (ATP).

Water potential A measure of the tendency of water molecules to move out of a solution. A more dilute solution has a higher (less negative) water potential. Water always moves from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential.

Root hair cells — adaptations

  • Large surface area (the long "hair" that grows out into the soil) — increases the rate of water and mineral ion absorption.
  • Many mitochondria — release energy from respiration for active transport of mineral ions.
  • No chloroplasts — root hair cells are underground and never receive light.

Rhizobium bacteria live in nodules on legume roots. They fix nitrogen from the air into nitrogen compounds the plant can use, and the plant supplies them with sugars in return — a mutualistic relationship.

Your coursebook's own labelled diagram of this cell (Fig 9.7 B) also marks the cell wall, cell membrane and a large (permanent) vacuole — the vacuole helps keep the cell turgid, which along with the wall gives it support even underground.

9.6 The use of fertilisers

Replacing minerals in the soil

Definition A fertiliser is any material of natural or synthetic origin that is applied to soil or plant tissue to supply one or more of the nutrients essential for plant growth.

Forms of fertiliser

  • Granular — solid grains scattered onto the soil; water-soluble, so they dissolve as they are watered/rained on.
  • Powdered — dissolved in water before being fed to the soil.
  • Liquid — a concentrated solution, diluted with water before use.

Artificial vs natural fertilisers

ArtificialNatural (organic / manure)
Chemicals made in factoriesNatural substances — dead, decaying plant/animal matter
No humusContains humus, which improves soil structure and water retention
Rich in plant nutrientsLess concentrated in nutrients
Absorbed quicklyAbsorbed slowly, as it decomposes
CostlyCost-effective
Can harm organisms; health risks if overused; can pollute waterwaysGenerally no harm to organisms

Science applied to industry — the Haber–Bosch process

The air is about 80% nitrogen, but most plants cannot use it directly — they need it as nitrate in the soil. In the early 20th century, German chemist Fritz Haber worked out how to combine nitrogen from the air with hydrogen to make ammonia, a nitrate-rich fertiliser — but only in small laboratory amounts. Chemist and engineer Carl Bosch then scaled Haber's reaction up to industrial size. The Haber–Bosch process is still used today, producing around 120 million tonnes of nitrate fertiliser a year, and is credited with helping feed a fast-growing world population — scientists estimate almost 50% of people alive today rely on food grown with the help of artificial fertilisers.

Worked example — fertiliser dose vs crop yield

Your coursebook gives real yield data for canola (oilseed rape) at different doses of nitrate fertiliser:

  • At 90 kg/ha: (3700 − 1250) ÷ 1250 × 100 ≈ 196% increase

A companion graph for wheat shows relative yield rising steeply at first as fertiliser is added, then levelling off and even dipping slightly past about 150–160 kg/ha — a good reminder that "more fertiliser" does not mean "endlessly more yield"; beyond a point, another factor limits growth and excess fertiliser is just wasted (or washed away).

Go deeper — NPK and eutrophication

Commercial fertilisers are often labelled with an N‑P‑K ratio: Nitrogen (leaf/stem growth), Phosphorus (root growth), Kalium/potassium (flowering, fruiting, and disease resistance). This ratio is general chemistry/agriculture knowledge rather than something in this particular coursebook chapter, but it is useful context for "suggest why a farmer might choose a specific fertiliser" questions.

Excess artificial fertiliser can wash (leach) into rivers and lakes, causing eutrophication: algae bloom, block light to plants below, then die and are decomposed by bacteria that use up dissolved oxygen — killing fish and other aquatic organisms.

9.7 Water and mineral transport in plants

Leaf structure, stomata, and transpiration

Water is absorbed from the soil into root hair cells by diffusion, moves into the xylem, travels up the stem, and evaporates out through the leaves — the whole pathway your coursebook calls the water and mineral transport route. Dissolved mineral ions such as nitrate travel the same route, carried along in the water.

External leaf anatomy — the parts you can see

Before looking inside the leaf, it helps to name what you can see from the outside:

  • Lamina — the broad, flat blade of the leaf, which maximises surface area for light absorption and gas exchange.
  • Midrib — the thick central vein running down the middle of the leaf.
  • Veins — a branching network (containing xylem and phloem) that supplies every part of the lamina with water and removes the sugars made there.
  • Margin — the outer edge of the leaf.
  • Petiole — the stalk that attaches the leaf to the stem.

Inside, a leaf's structure is arranged for efficient photosynthesis and gas exchange, layer by layer:

  • Cuticle — waxy, waterproof layer that reduces water loss.
  • Upper epidermis — transparent, single-cell layer that lets light through.
  • Palisade mesophyll — tightly packed, column-shaped cells with many chloroplasts; the main site of photosynthesis.
  • Spongy mesophyll — irregularly shaped cells with large air spaces between them, allowing gases to diffuse easily.
  • Lower epidermis — contains most of the stomata.

A note on wording: a single pore is a stoma (plural stomata), and the pair of cells that opens and closes it are the guard cells — not the same thing, even though it is an easy mix-up to make. Seen down a microscope at ×1000 magnification, stomata appear as narrow, lens-shaped slits scattered across the lower epidermis.

Gas exchange through an open stoma — the full cycle Stoma opens → carbon dioxide diffuses in → reaches the chloroplasts in the mesophyll → water is split and combined with CO₂ to build glucose → glucose is stored (as starch) or used for food and growth → oxygen, the by-product, diffuses back out through the same open stoma.

Guard cells and stomatal opening

StateWhat happensEffect
TurgidWater moves into the vacuole by osmosis; turgor pressure builds up; guard cells swell/curve apartStoma opens
FlaccidWater leaves the vacuole; the cell loses turgor pressure and shrinksStoma closes

Seeing the xylem work — the celery dye experiment

A simple way to prove water travels up the xylem: stand celery stems (which have many visible xylem tubes) in water mixed with food colouring, and leave for several hours. Coloured lines appear running up the stem, showing exactly where the xylem tubes are. As a control, one stem can have its cut end sealed with wax before standing it in the coloured water — no dye enters, which confirms the dye is travelling in through the cut xylem tubes themselves rather than soaking in some other way.

Transpiration The loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata. The continuous one-way flow of water it creates — from roots, up through the xylem, and out through the leaves — is called the transpiration stream. As water evaporates from the leaf cells and diffuses out, more water is pulled up through the plant behind it — rather like sucking on a drinking straw.
Go deeper — factors affecting transpiration rate
  • Light intensity ↑ → stomata open wider → transpiration rate ↑
  • Temperature ↑ → more evaporation and faster diffusion of water vapour → rate ↑
  • Humidity ↑ → smaller water potential gradient between leaf and air → rate ↓
  • Wind speed ↑ → removes water vapour from the leaf surface faster → rate ↑

9.8 Xylem, phloem and plant pests

The two transport tissues, side by side

FeatureXylemPhloem
TransportsWater & mineral ionsSugars (mainly sucrose) — translocation
DirectionOne-way: roots → leaves (up)Both ways: source → sink
CellsDead, hollow, tube-likeLiving — sieve tube elements + companion cells
WallsStrengthened with ligninThin cellulose walls, perforated end plates
Extra roleProvides structural support—

Phloem's living sieve tube cells are joined end to end at sieve plates — end walls with holes in them — which is what allows the sugary sap to flow both up and down the plant as needed, unlike the one-way, dead xylem tubes.

Plant pests that attack the phloem

Insects that feed on phloem sap are among the most damaging plant pests worldwide — individually tiny, but devastating in the huge numbers they occur in. They include aphids, plant hoppers, leafhoppers, cicadas, spittlebugs, scale insects and shield bugs.

All of them feed using a stylet — a sharp, hollow, needle-like mouthpart that is pushed through the outer tissues of a stem or leaf straight into the phloem, letting the insect drink the sugar-rich sap directly.

Two ways pests damage plants 1. Directly — stealing sugars from the phloem (or eating parts of the plant itself) reduces the resources a plant has for growth, flowers and fruit. 2. Indirectly — many phloem-feeders also carry plant pathogens (viruses, bacteria or fungi) on their stylets. As they feed, they inject pathogens straight into the transport tissues, which then get carried all over the plant. If a pathogen blocks the xylem, water cannot reach the stem and leaves, and the plant wilts and can die.
Go deeper — why ring-barking kills a tree

In trees, the xylem and phloem run in a layer just under the bark. If an animal (or a gardener's strimmer) strips away a complete ring of bark around the trunk, every phloem tube in that ring is cut. Sugars made in the leaves can no longer reach the roots, which starve and die — even though water can often still travel up the intact inner xylem for a while. This is why a "girdled" tree usually dies slowly, roots first.

Command words in IGCSE Biology

Exactly what each instruction word is asking you to do

Examiners choose command words carefully — matching your answer style to the word used is one of the fastest ways to pick up marks that are otherwise lost to a mismatch (e.g. writing a "describe" answer to an "explain" question).

Command wordWhat it wants
State / NameA short, precise answer with no explanation — a fact, term, or example.
DefineThe precise meaning of a term.
DescribeSay what happens — the pattern, sequence, or features you can observe — without explaining why.
ExplainSay why it happens — the reasons, causes, or mechanisms behind an observation.
CompareIdentify similarities and differences between two things — a table often helps.
SuggestApply your biology knowledge to a new/unfamiliar situation — there may be more than one acceptable answer.
CalculateShow your working and give a final numerical answer, with units where relevant.
EvaluateWeigh up evidence or a method, considering strengths and limitations, to reach a judgement.
Quick habit Underline the command word and the number of marks before you start writing. One mark usually = one clear point — a 3-mark "explain" question needs three distinct linked ideas, not one idea said three ways.
