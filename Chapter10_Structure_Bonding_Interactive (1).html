10.1Structure, bonding and properties

Proton number and the periodic table

Define proton number (atomic number) Explain how proton number fixes an element's place in the periodic table

Every atom is built around a nucleus made of two kinds of particle: positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. No two elements ever share the same number of protons — that count is the one thing that makes an element what it is. Chemists call it the proton number, though you'll just as often see it called the atomic number.

Because proton number is such a reliable fingerprint, the entire periodic table is arranged around it. Start at hydrogen and move along a row, then drop to the next: the proton number always climbs by exactly one at a time, in an unbroken sequence from 1 all the way up past 118.

Key points

  • Proton number (atomic number) is the number of protons in an atom's nucleus.
  • Every element has its own unique proton number.
  • The periodic table lists elements in order of increasing proton number.

10.2Structure, bonding and properties

Electrons in atoms

Define electron configuration Draw the electron configuration of elements 1–20

A neutral atom always carries exactly as many electrons as protons. Those electrons don't swarm randomly around the nucleus — they sit in defined layers called shells, filling the shell nearest the nucleus first. At this level, the rule is simple: the first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons, the second holds up to 8, and the third can in principle hold up to 18, though for the elements you'll meet here it typically settles at 8 before a new outer shell begins.

The way an atom's electrons are shared out across its shells is called its electron configuration. It's usually written as a short list of numbers — sodium, for example, has the configuration 2,8,1.

Key points

  • Electrons occupy shells around the nucleus, filling the innermost shell first.
  • Maximum shell size at this level: 1st shell = 2, 2nd shell = 8, 3rd shell = 8 (up to 18 overall).
  • An electron configuration such as 2,8,1 records how many electrons sit in each shell.

Practice questions

1. How many electrons are there in a neutral atom of fluorine?
2. Write the electron configuration of potassium.
3. Use the shell builder above to draw aluminium's electron configuration. What do you notice about its outer shell?
Aluminium is 2,8,3 — its outer shell holds only 3 electrons, far short of a full shell, which is why it's easy for aluminium to lose them.
4. Two different atoms both have 8 electrons in their outer shell. What do they have in common chemically?
Both have a full outer shell, so both are stable and largely unreactive — this is the arrangement found in the noble gases.
10.3Structure, bonding and properties

Making ions

Define ion Describe how ions form Explain why atoms form ions Write formulae for simple ions

An ion is simply an atom that has become electrically charged by losing or gaining electrons. Since electrons carry negative charge, losing one or more leaves behind more protons than electrons — a positive ion. Gaining electrons does the reverse, producing a negative ion.

Metal atoms usually have very few outer electrons, so they lose them and become positive ions. Non-metal atoms usually have nearly-full outer shells, so they gain electrons and become negative ions. Either way, atoms form ions for the same reason: to reach a full, stable outer shell — the same arrangement found in the noble gases.

Writing ion formulae

An ion's formula is written as its chemical symbol with the charge shown as a superscript on the right — for example Na+ or Cl−. The number goes before the sign for charges bigger than 1, e.g. Mg2+.

Key points

  • An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) that has lost or gained electrons and so carries a charge.
  • Losing electrons gives a positive ion; gaining electrons gives a negative ion.
  • Atoms form ions in order to reach a full, stable outer shell.
  • An ion's formula shows its symbol and its charge, e.g. K+, O2−.

Practice questions

1. Define the term ion, in your own words.
A charged particle, formed when a neutral atom loses or gains one or more electrons.
2. Write the formula for each ion: a potassium ion with charge +1; a magnesium ion with charge +2; a bromide ion with charge −1.
3. A fluorine atom has 7 outer electrons. What charge will its ion have, and why?
−1. It only needs one more electron to complete its outer shell, so it gains a single electron and ends up with one more electron than protons.
4. Use the ion maker above to model calcium reacting with chlorine. What are the charges on the two ions formed?
A calcium ion, Ca2+, and a chloride ion, Cl− (two chloride ions actually form for every calcium ion, since calcium has two electrons to give away).
10.4Structure, bonding and properties

Inside ionic compounds

Define ionic bonding, ionic compound and giant ionic structure Link the giant ionic structure model to physical properties

Once ions have formed, opposite charges attract. That electrostatic pull between positive and negative ions is called ionic bonding. Because the attraction acts equally in every direction, it doesn't stop at linking just two ions — it locks millions of them into a repeating three-dimensional arrangement known as a giant ionic structure. A compound built this way, from a metal and a non-metal, is an ionic compound.

Interactive · giant ionic lattice
A flat slice through a giant ionic lattice — larger ions in one colour, smaller in the other, packed edge to edge in a repeating pattern.

This model explains the physical properties of ionic compounds rather neatly. The attraction between ions is strong and reaches through the whole structure, so pulling the lattice apart takes a great deal of energy — which is why ionic compounds tend to have high melting points. It also explains why they're brittle: a sharp knock can shift one row of ions out of line, so ions that used to face an opposite charge suddenly meet one of the same charge. The repulsion that follows splits the crystal cleanly along that plane.

Key points

  • Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely-charged ions.
  • It produces a giant, repeating three-dimensional lattice.
  • The strong attraction throughout the lattice gives ionic compounds high melting points.
  • A shifted row of ions can bring like charges together, which repel and split the crystal — this is why ionic solids are brittle.

Practice questions

1. Write definitions for ionic bonding, ionic compound, and giant ionic structure.
Ionic bonding: the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Ionic compound: a substance made of positive and negative ions, usually a metal combined with a non-metal. Giant ionic structure: the repeating 3-D pattern that ionic bonding locks the ions into.
2. Explain why ionic compounds have high melting points.
The electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions is strong and acts throughout the whole lattice, so a large amount of energy is needed to overcome it and separate the ions.
3. Explain why ionic compounds are brittle rather than bendy.
A force can shift one layer of ions relative to the next, lining up ions of the same charge. Those like charges repel strongly, so the layers push apart and the crystal shatters.
4. A physical model — like the lattice above, or one built from fruit — can show ion positions clearly. Give one thing such a model fails to capture.
It doesn't show that the ions are actually vibrating on the spot, and it can't easily show that the electrostatic attraction pulls equally in every direction, not just towards each visible neighbour.
10.5Structure, bonding and properties

Covalent bonding

Define covalent bond Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for simple molecules

Not every atom completes its outer shell by trading electrons outright. Two non-metal atoms can instead share a pair of electrons — each atom counts the shared pair as part of its own outer shell, so both atoms complete their shells at the same time. That shared pair is a covalent bond, and a particle built from atoms joined this way is a molecule.

Chemists show exactly which electrons are shared using a dot-and-cross diagram: one atom's own electrons are drawn as dots, the other atom's as crosses. That makes it easy to check that every atom ends up with a full outer shell — and easy to see which atom each shared electron originally came from.

Key points

  • A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons that joins two atoms.
  • Non-metal atoms share electrons so each atom gains a full outer shell.
  • A dot-and-cross diagram shows which atom each electron in the diagram came from.

Practice questions

1. Define the term covalent bond.
A shared pair of electrons that holds two atoms together.
2. How many shared pairs of electrons are there in a molecule of methane, CH₄?
3. A chlorine atom has 7 outer electrons. How many more does it need to complete its outer shell, and how does it get them in Cl₂?
Just 1 more. In Cl₂, each chlorine atom shares one electron with the other, forming a single covalent bond — that shared pair completes both atoms' outer shells.
