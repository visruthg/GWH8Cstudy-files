Plate Tectonics & Earthquakes

An interactive study guide covering the basics, plus the Christchurch and Haiti earthquake case studies — with a labelling activity and a quiz at the end.

The ground beneath your feet is made of huge slabs of rock — tectonic plates — that are always moving, very slowly. Where they meet is where most earthquakes and volcanoes happen.

Structure of the Earth

Earth is made of layers. The crust and the rigid uppermost mantle together form the lithosphere — this is broken into the tectonic plates. The plates float on the soft, partly-melted asthenosphere beneath them.

Three ways plates meet

Heat from deep inside the Earth drives convection currents in the mantle, which slowly move the plates apart, together, or alongside one another.

1 · DIVERGENT — plates move apart

New crust is created as magma rises to fill the gap. On the ocean floor this builds a ridge (e.g. the Mid-Atlantic Ridge); on land it forms a rift valley (e.g. East Africa).

2 · CONVERGENT — plates move together

Where dense oceanic crust meets lighter continental crust, the oceanic plate is forced under (subducted) and melts, sometimes forming volcanoes.

3 · TRANSFORM — plates slide past each other

No crust is created or destroyed, but friction builds up as the plates grind past one another — released suddenly as an earthquake (e.g. the San Andreas Fault, and the fault behind the Haiti earthquake).

Focus and epicentre

The focus is the point underground where the stress is released. The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it — the closer to it, the greater the shaking damage.

Measuring earthquakes

Strength is measured on the Richter Scale using a seismometer. The scale is exponential — each whole number is about ten times stronger than the one below it. A magnitude 5–6 quake usually causes limited disruption; 6–8 can be disastrous; 8+ can be catastrophic.

New Zealand sits between the Pacific and Australian Plates. Two major earthquakes struck the Christchurch area within six months of each other — with very different outcomes.

Haiti sits on a transform boundary between the Caribbean Plate and the North American Plate. On 12 January 2010, a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck near the capital, Port-au-Prince.
