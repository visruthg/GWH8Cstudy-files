CHAPTER 1

Mr. Jones, of the Manor Farm, had locked the hen-houses for the night, but was too drunk to remember to shut the pop-holes. Mr Jones - Careless With the ring of light from his lantern dancing from side to side, he lurched across the yard, kicked off his boots at the back door, drew himself a last glass of beer from the barrel in the scullery and made his way up to bed, where Mrs. Jones was already snoring.

As soon as the light in the bedroom went out there was a stirring and a fluttering all through the farm buildings. Word had gone round during the day that old Major, the prize Middle White boar, had had a strange dream on the previous night and wished to communicate it to the other animals. It had been agreed that they should all meet in the big barn as soon as Mr. Jones was safely out of the way. Old Major (so he was always called, though the name under which he had been exhibited was Willingdon Beauty) was so highly regarded on the farm Regarded that everyone was quite ready to lose an hour's sleep in order to hear what he had to say.

At one end of the big barn, on a sort of raised platform, Major was already ensconced on his bed of straw, under a lantern which hung from a beam. He was twelve years old and had lately grown rather stout, but he was still a majestic-looking pig, with a wise and benevolent appearance Major - Wise, Majestic in spite of the fact that his tushes had never been cut. Before long the other animals began to arrive and make themselves comfortable after their different fashions. First came the three dogs, Bluebell, Jessie, and Pincher, and then the pigs, who settled down in the straw immediately in front of the platform...

The two cart-horses, Boxer and Clover, came in together, walking very slowly and setting down their vast hairy hoofs with great care lest there should be some small animal concealed in the straw. Clover was a stout motherly mare approaching middle life... Boxer was an enormous beast, nearly eighteen hands high, and as strong as any two ordinary horses put together... he was universally respected for his steadiness of character and tremendous powers of work. After the horses came Muriel, the white goat, and Benjamin, the donkey. Benjamin was the oldest animal on the farm, and the worst tempered. He seldom talked, and when he did, it was usually to make some cynical remark...

[ Major's Address to the Animals ]

"Now, comrades, what is the nature of this life of ours? Let us face it: our lives are miserable, laborious, and short. We are born, we are given just so much food as will keep the breath in our bodies, and those of us who are capable of it are forced to work to the last atom of our strength; and the very instant that our usefulness has come to an end we are slaughtered with hideous cruelty. No animal in England knows the meaning of happiness or leisure after he is a year old. No animal in England is free. The life of an animal is misery and slavery: that is the plain truth.

"...Man is the only real enemy we have. Remove Man from the scene, and the root cause of hunger and overwork is abolished for ever. Man is the only creature that consumes without producing..."

[ 'Beasts of England' ]

The singing of this song threw the animals into the wildest excitement. Almost before Major had reached the end, they had begun singing it for themselves... And then, after a few preliminary tries, the whole farm burst out into Beasts of England in tremendous unison. The cows lowed it, the dogs whined it, the sheep bleated it, the horses whinnied it, the ducks quacked it. They were so delighted with the song that they sang it right through five times in succession, and might have continued singing it all night if they had not been interrupted.

Unfortunately, the uproar awoke Mr. Jones, who sprang out of bed, making sure that there was a fox in the yard. He seized the gun which always stood in a corner of his bedroom, and let fly a charge of number 6 shot into the darkness. The pellets buried themselves in the wall of the barn and the meeting broke up hurriedly.

Literary devices at work

Key techniques in Orwell's opening chapters for analysis and essay practice.

Cataloguing of animal sounds (Onomatopoeia & Parallelism)

"The cows lowed it, the dogs whined it, the sheep bleated it, the horses whinnied it, the ducks quacked it."

Orwell gives each species its own precise sound verb. The rhythmic list structure mimics how collective revolutionary ideas sweep across diverse groups, creating immediate but uncritical unity.

Character Allegory & Characterisation

"too drunk to remember to shut the pop-holes" / "wise and benevolent appearance"

Jones represents the decay of Tsar Nicholas II's regime through careless cruelty and alcoholism. Old Major represents Karl Marx/Lenin, possessing moral authority and ideological vision.

Anticlimax / Bathos

"...the whole farm was asleep in a moment."

The euphoria of singing 'Beasts of England' is shattered instantly by a single gunshot, demonstrating how fragile idealism is when confronted by autocratic force.

Anaphora and Rhetorical Rule of Three

"our lives are miserable, laborious, and short."

Old Major utilizes persuasive triadic structure to distill complex systemic oppression into clear, unforgettable emotional truths for his audience.

Key moments to know

Essential narrative pivot points in Chapters 1 & 2.

01

Mr. Jones's Drunken Carelessness

Jones forgets to close the pop-holes, showing his neglect and establishing the justification for animal rebellion.

Why it matters: It establishes human tyranny not as competent authority, but as abusive negligence.

02

Old Major's Speech & The Anthem

Old Major outlines the principles of Animalism and teaches 'Beasts of England'.

Why it matters: Plants the philosophical roots of rebellion and collective solidarity before pigs corrupt the message.

03

The Gunshot into the Barn

Jones fires into the dark, scattering the meeting.

Why it matters: Foreshadows future armed conflicts (Battle of the Cowshed) and violent suppression of speech.

Literary devices at work

Comprehensive analysis of Shelley's poetic techniques.

Apostrophe

"O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being"

Shelley addresses an inanimate natural force directly as a living entity, elevating the poem into an urgent prayerful invocation.

Oxymoron & Paradox

"Destroyer and Preserver; hear, O hear!"

Distills the central motif: the wind destroys dead leaves/decaying ideas while preserving winged seeds for spring's rebirth.

Simile & Necromantic Imagery

"driven, like ghosts from an enchanter fleeing" / "Each like a corpse within its grave"

Transforms autumn leaves into spectral figures and corpses, emphasizing death as a necessary precursor to resurrection.

Classical Allusion & Mythological Metaphor

"bright hair uplifted... Of some fierce Maenad"

Compares storm clouds to the wild hair of a frenzied female worshipper of Dionysus, capturing violent natural energy.

Architectural & Funeral Metaphor

"dome of a vast sepulchre, Vaulted with all thy congregated might"

Envisions the night sky as a massive tomb built from clouds, framing the year's end with somber grandeur.

Stanza Summaries

Breakdown of Stanzas I & II structure.

I

Stanza I: Terrestrial Power (Earth & Seeds)

Drives dead leaves across the land and buries seeds underground until Spring's trumpet awakens them.

Why it matters: Introduces the dual role of Nature as both destructive and life-giving.

II

Stanza II: Atmospheric Power (Sky & Storms)

Carries storm clouds through the sky like falling leaves, brewing rain, lightning, and hail.

Why it matters: Expands the scale from terrestrial leaves to cosmic weather patterns.

Shaw's dramatic and comedic techniques in Act I.

Dramatic Foil & Ideological Contrast

Raina's "heroic ideals" vs. Bluntschli's "duty to live as long as we can"

Bluntschli acts as a direct pragmatic foil to Raina's romanticized, opera-derived concepts of warfare.

Symbolic Set Design

"half rich Bulgarian, half cheap Viennese"

Reflects the clash between local Bulgarian culture and superficial Western romantic imports.

Bathos / Anti-climax

"I always carry chocolate instead of cartridges"

Undercuts high-stakes military tension with mundane domestic realities, puncturing romantic heroism.

[P] Shaw makes this moment striking by staging a direct collision between Raina's romantic fantasy of war and its blunt, pragmatic reality. [E] When Raina claims soldiers are "afraid of death," the soldier responds bluntly: "All of them, dear lady... It is our duty to live as long as we can." [E] Shaw uses this sharp dialogue contrast to dismantle romanticized heroism. The officer's unvarnished survivalism punctures Raina's ideal of tragic nobility, reframing courage not as reckless sacrifice but as practical endurance. [L] This interaction establishes Shaw's overarching satirical message: real human value lies in self-aware pragmatism rather than dangerous romantic delusion.
